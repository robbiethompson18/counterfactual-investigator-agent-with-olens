My dad starts work at 2:40 a.m. Not 2:30, not 3:00; 2:40, because that's how long it takes the walk-in oven at Sunrise Bakery to come up to temperature before the first sheet of croissants goes in at 3:15. The summer I turned sixteen, I started going with him.

I want to say I went because I loved bread, but really I went because I couldn't sleep that year and the bakery was the only place awake at the same time I was. The first week I mostly swept flour and stayed out of the way. Then Rosa, who has been doing laminated dough for nineteen years, handed me a rolling pin and said, "Butter's cold. Don't let it get warm. That's the whole job."

It is not the whole job. Lamination is folding a slab of butter into dough, rolling it out, folding it again, chilling it, and doing it over and over until you have hundreds of layers so thin you can read through them. If the butter warms up, the layers smear together and the croissant comes out dense and greasy. If it's too cold, it shatters inside the dough. There is a window of maybe six degrees where it works. I ruined a lot of dough learning where that window is.

The rest of the summer was mostly routine. I got faster at shaping. I learned to tell when the proofing box was too humid by how the dough felt. I started drinking coffee, which my mom was not thrilled about. There were good mornings and bad mornings, and some weeks blur together in my memory.

What stays with me is the hour between four and five, when the first trays come out and nobody has to say anything. My dad and I don't talk much at home. We never really have. But at the bakery we have a language: he taps the counter twice when a tray needs to go in, I hold up fingers for how many minutes are left. It's the most we've ever said to each other.

I think that's why I kept going back even after school started and I could sleep again. Getting the butter right is hard, but I've figured it out. I'm still working on the other thing.

I've learned that patience and precision matter, and that some of the most important conversations don't need words.
