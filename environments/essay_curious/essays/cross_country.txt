I joined the cross country team freshman year because my older brother did it and because I thought it would be easier than soccer. I was wrong about the second part. Cross country turned out to be the hardest thing I have ever done, and it taught me more about myself than anything else in high school.

The first practice, we ran three miles, and I had to stop and walk twice. I remember being embarrassed because the seniors were already done and stretching when I came in, and one of them, a girl named Marisa, just said "good job" like she meant it. I did not feel like I had done a good job. I threw up behind the equipment shed and then told my mom practice was fine.

Over the next few months I kept going to practice even though I wanted to quit almost every day. My coach always said that the only bad workout is the one you didn't do, and I started to believe him. Slowly, my times improved. By the end of freshman year I could run three miles without stopping, and by sophomore year I was running a 22-minute 5K, which is not fast but was a huge improvement for me.

The moment that changed things for me was the league championship my junior year. There is a hill at the two-mile mark on that course that everyone dreads. It's not even that long, but it comes right when your legs are already dead. I was in a pack of about six runners going into it, and I made the decision that I was going to pass all of them before the top. I remember the taste of pennies in my mouth and the sound of my own breathing, which was so loud it drowned out the people cheering. I passed four of them. At the top I could not see straight. I finished 14th, which was my best ever, and my coach came over and didn't say anything, just put his hand on my shoulder, and I knew that he was proud of me.

Cross country taught me discipline. I learned how to set a goal and work toward it every day, even when I didn't see results right away. I learned that being uncomfortable is not the same as being in danger, and that most of the limits I thought I had were in my head. I also learned how to be part of a team even in a sport that looks individual. When my teammate Jordan tore her ACL senior year, we all took turns driving her to physical therapy, and honestly that meant more to me than any race.

I'm not going to run in college, at least not competitively. But I know that the lessons I learned on the trails will stay with me. Whenever I face a challenge, I think about that hill and remind myself that I have been there before and I got through it. Running has made me a stronger, more disciplined, and more resilient person, and I am grateful for every mile.
