My mother writes English on the inside of her left wrist. Not whole sentences, just the words she is afraid of: "deductible," "appointment," "Wednesday." She writes them in pen the night before, and by lunchtime they have smudged into a blue bruise, and she holds her wrist up to the light at the pharmacy counter like she's checking the time.

For most of middle school I pretended not to know her in public. That is the honest version. The version I told myself was that I was "letting her practice," but the truth is that when the woman at the DMV looked over my mother's head and asked me, "What is she trying to say?" I was relieved to be the one who got asked.

Communication is one of the most important skills a person can have. In today's world, being able to express yourself clearly is essential in every field, from medicine to business to the arts. I have always valued communication and I believe it is the key to understanding between people of different backgrounds.

My mother came here from Lima when she was twenty-six with an accounting degree that nobody would accept. She worked at a dry cleaner for six years and now she does the books for three restaurants owned by people from our church, all of whom speak Spanish, which is partly why her English stopped improving. She says this without any bitterness. She also says the dry cleaner was the best job she ever had because the owner let her bring me and I slept in a laundry basket behind the counter, which I don't remember but which explains, she says, why I like the smell of steam.

The belief I had to challenge was that her English was her problem. I believed it for a long time without ever saying it out loud, which is how you know a belief is really yours. I think I started to question it in tenth grade, when my Spanish teacher, who was from Ohio, mispronounced a word and the whole class let it go, and I thought about eleven years of people not letting it go for my mom.

Now when we go somewhere I stand next to her instead of a step in front. If someone looks at me instead of her, I look at her too, so there is nowhere else for them to look. It sounds small. It took me years. Sometimes she still gets frustrated at how slow a conversation is going and her frustration at that is also something that I have had to learn to not try and fix for her, which is hard.

I'm not going to pretend I don't still get embarrassed sometimes. I do. But it's a different embarrassment now, one that is pointed at myself instead of at her.

She still writes on her wrist. Last week it said "orthodontist."
