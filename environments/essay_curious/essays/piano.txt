The piano in our living room has been out of tune since before I was born. My mom bought it from a church that was closing for forty dollars and the cost of renting a truck, and the one time she called a tuner he quoted her two hundred and said the pinblock was probably cracked and it might not hold anyway. So it sat there, a little flat in the middle and badly sharp at the top, and I learned to play on it, which means I learned to play slightly wrong.

Sophomore year I found a video of a man in Ohio tuning an upright with a wrench that had a star-shaped head. It was forty minutes long and he barely talked. I watched it three times, then ordered the wrench and a bag of rubber mutes for eleven dollars.

Here is what nobody tells you: every note above the bass is actually two or three strings, and they all have to agree with each other before you even think about whether they agree with the rest of the piano. You wedge a mute between two of them so only one rings, you hit the key, and you listen for the beats, this slow wah-wah-wah that gets slower as the strings get closer and finally disappears. Then you pull the mute and do the next one. It is unbelievably boring to describe and I can't stop doing it.

The first time I tried, I spent two and a half hours on a single octave and came out of it with my ears ringing and my left hand cramped around the wrench. My mom came home from work and asked if I'd eaten. I had not eaten. I had also tuned the middle C unison so perfectly that when I hit it, it sounded like one string instead of three, like the note had gotten heavier, and I made her come listen and she said "okay" in the voice she uses for memes.

The thing I've come to love is that the piano fights you. A string you tune on Tuesday has drifted by Saturday. The pinblock is in fact a little cracked, so the A above middle C will never quite hold, and I have come to think of it as a pet with a chronic condition. You don't fix a piano. You negotiate with it, and you lose a little every time. I have tuned it eleven times now. It has never once been in tune. Sometimes I think it is the only thing in the house that tells me the truth.

I also found out that a "perfectly" tuned piano isn't tuned to the math. If you make every fifth pure, the octaves come out wrong, so tuners make every interval a tiny bit off so that everything is equally, tolerably wrong. I think about this more than is normal. It's a whole instrument built on the idea that fairness means nobody gets exactly what they want.

Tuning the piano has taught me patience, persistence, and the value of listening closely. Whatever I end up studying, I will bring that same focus with me. In the end, I think that's what it's all about: not getting everything perfect, but showing up and trying again.
