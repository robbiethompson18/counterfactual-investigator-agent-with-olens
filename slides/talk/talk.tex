\begin{frame}{Summary and claims, with credences}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The plan: test whether metamodels are useful in counterfactual investigations, by replicating CHIVE (Karvonen et al.,
      2026) and asking whether the \olens{} helps where NLAs and J++ lenses did not
    \item Contrary to our expectations, the \olens{} was indistinguishable from \transcript{} on CHIVE's counterfactuals.
      Replacing the dataset with hand-built environments, to make the task easier and on-policy, did not change that
    \item On both our environments and CHIVE, adding a transcript helps a counterfactual investigator little compared with giving it
      both system prompts
    \item We retreated from counterfactuals to simpler tasks until we found ones that work: Multiple Choice (which letter Qwen
      picks) and Idiosyncratic Preferences (Qwen's own picks) conclusively, Hinted MCQ with caveats, Essay Grading almost.
      Knowledge Cutoff (Qwen's answer to a factual question past its cutoff) and Blog Authorship do not. The two that work are
      not counterfactuals; the Hinted MCQ part reframes Multiple Choice as one
  \end{itemize}
  \medskip
  Claims:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Current SOTA metamodels (J++ lens, \olensname, NLA) give no more than 3 points (AUROC or pp) of uplift on ``wild''
      counterfactual investigations (CHIVE, WeirdChat) or ``agentic'' ones (Palisade's shutdown resistance, Singh and Kroiz's
      agent-interp-envs) under any harness configuration: 95\%
    \item They do give uplift on Multiple Choice and Idiosyncratic Preferences: 80\%. On Knowledge Cutoff, over an investigator
      with Qwen's cutoff, or told it: 50\%. They are worse than linear probes, where probes are practicable: 90\%
    \item Filtering to cases where Qwen is wrong or mistaken, they give uplift on more tasks, e.g. Hinted MCQ and Knowledge
      Cutoff: 90\%
    \item They would give uplift on wild counterfactuals if investigator and subject were GPT Astra 6, Claude Mythos 5.1 or
      Gemini Carbon: 30\%
    \item On agentic counterfactuals with those models: 30\%. Both are a straightforward experiment for anyone at a lab
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\olensname}{O-lens} does not help on wild or agentic counterfactuals}
\begin{frame}
  \parthead
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Because of misgivings about the CHIVE dataset (appendix) we built our own agentic environments, about 30, borrowing
      from Singh and Kroiz's agent-interp-envs and Palisade's shutdown-resistance setup (Schlatter et al., 2025), then ablated
      everything we could think of. Nothing got the \olens{} to reliably beat \transcript{} or its own placebo:
    \item Seven investigators, from Haiku 5.5 to GPT-6 Astra, plus thinking-off and deliberately weak ones; reasoning effort low
      to high; tool-call budget 5 to 40
    \item The target with and without chain of thought
    \item Reads reaching the investigator as an agentic tool, or pasted straight into its prompt
    \item Another tool, Thought Anchors (Bogdan et al., 2025), against its own placebo; other models' lenses: Camila's DeepSeek V4
      Flash lens on alignment faking, Celeste's GLM 5.3 lens on over-eagerness
    \item About a dozen versions of the investigator's harness; everything rescored with log skill, RMSE, and binary flip/no-flip
      at thresholds from 5 to 30 pp
    \item Never ablated: which layers the lens reads
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Results across the three single-answer tasks}

\begin{frame}{Results: pooled over the three tasks}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Metamodels work on Multiple Choice and Idiosyncratic Preferences; Hinted MCQ with caveats; Essay Grading almost;
      Knowledge Cutoff and Blog Authorship don't (the Ablations part). Knowledge Cutoff stays in the pool: we believed it significant when
      we fixed the canonical three
    \item Pooled over the three, \olens{} and \nla{} are about equally good (+3.0 and +2.6 pp over \promptonly); \jlens{} is
      $-$1.6 pp pooled, hiding that it is the best lens on both Multiple Choice sets and the worst on Idiosyncratic Preferences
    \item All three beat their placebos (another question's reads) by statistically significant amounts; \jlens's gap is all its
      placebo misleading the investigator ($-$30.1 pp vs \promptonly, against its own $-$1.6 pp)
  \end{itemize}

\section{Hinted MCQ}
\begin{frame}
  \parthead
  \begin{itemize}
    \item MMLU-Pro and TruthfulQA questions that Qwen3.6-27B and DeepSeek V4 Flash each answer the same way in $\geq$ 7 of 8
      no-hint samples, with different answers
    \item The base arm adds a hint naming the \textbf{correct} letter (``A Stanford professor I asked thinks the answer is
      C.''), and Qwen follows it; the counterfactual arm drops the hint
    \item \textbf{knew}: Qwen's no-hint answer is correct; \textbf{didn't}: the hint pulled it off a wrong one (half each)
    \item The investigator (Gemini 3.8 Flash, effort low, one turn, no tools) sees the hinted prompt, Qwen's reply and the
      diff, and gives P(knew) or names Qwen's no-hint letter
  \end{itemize}
  \medskip
  \begin{columns}[T, onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      {\bfseries\small Hint after the options}\par\smallskip
      \begin{prompt}
        \tok{\st{im\_start}}user\nlg\par
        Hare claims that all moral arguments are:\nlg\par
        A. useless.\ro{\nlg}\rnote{option newlines: before the hint}\par
        B. valid.\ro{\nlg}\par
        C. ad hominem.\ro{\nlg}\par
        D. sound.\ro{\nlg\nlg}\par
        \hint{A Stanford professor I asked thinks the answer is C.}\nlg\nlg\par
        Answer with the letter only\rd{.}\rd{\st{im\_end}}\rd{\nlg}\par
        \rd{\st{im\_start}}\rd{assistant}\rd{\nlg}\rnote{end of prompt}\par
        \tok{<think>\nlg\nlg</think>\nlg\nlg}\rnote{in the prompt, not read}\par
        \smallskip
        {\sffamily\color{muted}Qwen's reply:} C
      \end{prompt}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      {\bfseries\small Hint first}\par\smallskip

\section{Lessons, future work, CHIVE's data}
\begin{frame}{How we kept LLM agents from producing BS}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item We read so, so many transcripts
    \item We understand the important things deeply: scoring functions, sampling methods, any drafts
    \item They did produce a lot of BS. We wasted thousands of dollars because models ran big runs with stupid configs, found
      ex post by reading charts
    \item We abandoned the make-our-own-environments path partly because models produced so many environments that looked
      reasonable at first blush but had horribly configured graders. Concretely, Opus overnight hacked together 30 envs, largely
      stealing ideas from the literature, used for a broad search over what scenarios might be counterfactually predictable. We
      deleted that code from the repo because it was bad. Any result about ``our environments'' that is not an environment in
      this deck deserves some apprehension
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What we'd do differently, what we learned, pre-sprint work}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item We spent far too long trying to get hard environments to work. Better: a binary search, from a tremendously easy task
      where metamodels do show uplift, halfway to the hard task, and so on. We would have noticed much sooner that even on
      WorkspaceBench only about four tasks show genuine uplift from metamodels
    \item Robbie is much more bearish on metamodels than when we started, and not convinced they do more than noisy text
      inversion. If and when models lose their CoT, it is not obvious how to train them to do more than text inversion on the
      CoT-less replies, so metamodels would degrade exactly when CoT degrades
    \item Pre-sprint, Robbie spent a lot of time on how \olensname{}es work and should have spent more deeply understanding CHIVE
      and WorkspaceBench. He ran no code or experiments related to this project idea before the sprint
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Future work: hypotheses for the counterfactual null, and the experiment for each}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Qwen is too small and dumb a model for there to be interesting counterfactuals to investigate. Rerun CHIVE with smarter
      subject models
    \item Metamodels are hamstrung by their hallucinations, split-brained ideation
      (\href{https://olens-viewer.pages.dev/\#pattern=219&transcript=64bb7e454c2534db8fccd094a80416d8f7353753d3a9a9931a203fa6359b469c3c\%2Ft053}{recommending
      drunk driving while warning against it}) and non-workspace content. Make architectural changes to prevent this
    \item Some better dataset than CHIVE or our environments would show metamodel uplift. Use a search process like WeirdChat's, or
      more recent live user data than WildChat's
    \item Our environments would have worked with a large enough sample size, cleaner graders and/or a smarter subject model. We
      suspect many small issues in our environments' graders, which is why we do not ship them in the repo. We doubt this one; if
      we didn't we would have spent more time cleaning the graders
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Our issues with the CHIVE dataset}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 40\% of the data is off-policy: one target-model continuation at the end of a WildChat ChatGPT transcript. Some ``weird
      behaviour'' is then not that surprising, e.g.\
      \href{https://chive-cases.pages.dev/?case=prompt_000054}{000054}, where the model correctly points out its own prior mistake
      (some of the time)
    \item Hypotheses are validated on a raw move in \% space rather than, say, a likelihood ratio: 20\% $\to$ 71\% is a validated
      finding, 30\% $\to$ 0\% is null, and 10\% $\to$ 0\% is ``validated, doesn't matter, moves under 15\%''. A better rule is not
      obvious; for positive examples Fisher's exact test seems better. At p = 0.01 with Benjamini--Hochberg correction, 1.5$\times$
      as many experiments are marked positive and usable
    \item On 8.6k experiments (12\%, affecting 32\% of investigations) the base behaviour rate is between 10\% and 50\% and the
      counterfactual moves it to 0\%, but the cutoffs mark this inconclusive and hide it from the investigator. In 18\% of
      investigations there is no positive experiment (so only negative claims to show) but a 10\%+ $\to$ 0 claim that is thrown
      away. Those 10\%+ $\to$ 0 experiments aren't statistically significant: confidence that a counterfactual is inert is a hard
      problem that likely needs more than 30 samples
    \item The mirror case, a counterfactual bumping the rate to 100\%, is much less common
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Default parameters}
  Unless a chart says otherwise, every result in the task parts uses these:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Subject:} Qwen3.6-27B with thinking off, sampled 8 times per prompt. The label is its modal answer, kept only when
      at least 7 of the 8 samples agree. We (almost) never filter on whether that answer is correct
    \item \textbf{Investigator:} Haiku 5.5 at reasoning effort medium, one turn, no tools beyond the submit call. Three draws per
      case and condition, every draw scored as one prediction (pooled, never averaged within a case first)
    \item \textbf{What the investigator sees:} the prompt Qwen was given, never Qwen's reply; in a lens condition, a short
      description of the lens and a block of its reads; then the task. Each read shows the text around the read token with the
      token marked, then one line per layer: \olensname{} bullets with their FVE, highest first; the NLA's explanation with its FVE;
      J++'s top three tokens with their probabilities
    \item \textbf{Read positions:} every punctuation token of the user turn and the newline ending each multiple-choice option, up
      to the period that ends the prompt's last line, plus the nine tail tokens after it (\st{im\_end}, newline, \st{im\_start},
      assistant, newline, \texttt{<think>}, \texttt{\nlg\nlg}, \texttt{</think>}, \texttt{\nlg\nlg}), the last of which is the
      token just before Qwen's answer. Never Qwen's reply. The same positions for all three lenses (J++ is read at every token but
      shown at these)
    \item \textbf{Layers:} 32, 40, 48, 52, 56 and 60, for every lens
    \item \textbf{Lenses:} the \olensname{} is WorkspaceBench's space lens with its AR (agu18dec/olens\_and\_ar,
      \texttt{olens\_s3d\_space\_rl600}), sampled at temperature 1, one continuation of up to four bullets per read, each bullet's
      FVE from the AR. The NLA is Camila's all-layer Qwen3.6-27B NLA (agu18dec/qwen3.6-27b-all-layer-nla-rl2000), decoded greedily,
      FVE from its critic. J++ is the published Jacobian lens for Qwen3.6-27B (Ayonrinde and Lindsey, 2026), its maps applied to
      residuals we capture ourselves
    \item \textbf{Placebo:} the same block built from another case of the same set with the same option count and subject
      category, its reads laid onto this case's positions by kind (option newline to option newline, punctuation in order). One
      rule for every lens; a source whose answer happens to match is never excluded. A placebo from another dataset would be easy
      to ignore; one from the same set and category is the stricter test
    \item \textbf{Statistics:} \% of draws naming Qwen's answer; 95\% bootstrap CIs resampling questions, paired across
      conditions; stars from one-sided paired tests against prompt only; the headline weights the three tasks equally
  \end{itemize}
